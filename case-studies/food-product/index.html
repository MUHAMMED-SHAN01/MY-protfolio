01

The Starting Point

While travelling in Thiruvananthapuram with friends, we wanted good-value food within a limited budget. A mandi might cost around ₹250–₹300 at one restaurant, while another might offer a mandi or combo around ₹150–₹200.

“Where can I find a good food offer near me right now?”

The problem was not necessarily that offers did not exist. I searched Instagram for food offers in Trivandrum, but many results were older videos or reels. Finding a current offer that matched our budget and location was difficult.

The practical alternatives were to search individual restaurants, ask people, or choose somewhere nearby.

PERSONAL OBSERVATION

Discovering an offer and reaching an open business were separate problems. After seeing an offer around Nedumangad, I later struggled to find the original reel, locate the shop, and on one occasion found it closed.

INITIAL PROBLEM

Students and young people looking for affordable food may struggle to discover relevant, currently available offers near their location through channels such as social media.

02

The Initial Assumption

ASSUMPTION

“People need a platform to find food offers near them.”

The first idea was a location-based platform showing nearby food offers. That assumption needed exploration on both sides of the marketplace.

USER SIDE

Students / young people

Discover nearby offers → Compare → Filter → Choose → Navigate

An initial target segment, not a statistically validated market segment.

MERCHANT SIDE

Food businesses

Publish offers → Attract customers → Promote existing offers → Potentially sell remaining food

Proposed product flow

  1. Current location
  2. Nearby offers
  3. Filter
  4. Choose
  5. Offer details
  6. Navigate

04

Merchant Discovery

Approximately 5 food businesses

Conversations included smaller and larger food businesses. Responses differed, and merchant needs were not identical.

MORE CUSTOMERS

Some businesses were interested in attracting nearby customers, including through exclusive offers.

PROMOTE EXISTING OFFERS

Some wanted to show offers they already provided to more potential customers.

REMAINING FOOD

Some businesses were more concerned about selling remaining food.

MERCHANT INSIGHT

One larger restaurant said leftover food was not its main problem: it could estimate demand, prepare more if needed, and handle remaining food through existing arrangements. Its more relevant interest was attracting customers.

Different businesses can have different problems even when they sell similar products.

05

Business Model Hypothesis

MERCHANT-PROPOSED HYPOTHESIS

NOT VALIDATED THROUGH A LIVE EXPERIMENT

  1. Offer an exclusive discount, such as 10%, 15%, or 20%
  2. Promote the offer through the platform or group
  3. Track customers attributed to the offer over about one month
  4. Demonstrate customer value to the business
  5. Explore potential paid membership

This approach was suggested by one shop owner as a way to test whether the platform could generate value. It is not evidence of willingness to pay.

06

The Marketplace Problem

MORE USERS↔MORE MERCHANTS

MERCHANT ASKS

“Are there enough users?”

USER ASKS

“Are there enough useful offers?”

If there are few merchants, users have fewer reasons to use the platform. If there are few users, merchants have less reason to maintain offers. Both sides need value for the product to work.

07

Offer Accuracy: The Operational Problem

Keeping listings accurate could require merchants to update quantities, prices, availability, and sold-out status throughout the day.

PLATFORM MAY STILL SHOW: 5 AVAILABLE

A customer could travel to the business and discover the offer is already finished.

Small businesses may not want to repeatedly open an app and update a few remaining portions. They may prefer to offer a small discount directly to customers instead. The product question therefore became how to provide timely, trustworthy information without adding excessive merchant work.

08

Why I Didn't Run a Live MVP

PROJECT LIMITATION

Some merchants were willing to provide offers, but I did not have an established local user base or enough contacts to distribute offers and measure actual customer visits reliably.

Without that distribution, I could not test the full path from merchant offer to user discovery, business visit, and merchant-measured result. I did not claim MVP validation without conducting that test.

YIP

College innovation program recognition
Presented the idea through YIP, received first prize, a cash prize, and a certificate.
Recognition of the idea and presentation, not proof of market demand, usage, or willingness to pay.

09

Scope Decision & Feedback

INITIAL PRODUCT SCOPE

Food offers

The initial product remained focused on food.

FUTURE EXPANSION HYPOTHESIS

Budget-friendly stays

Teachers and lecturers suggested accommodation as a possible future category for travellers and small providers. It was not part of the initial product.

First understand whether the food-offer marketplace can work before expanding into another category.

10

What I Actually Learned

OBSERVATIONS & FEEDBACK

  • Social media discovery can be difficult when content is old or hard to find.
  • Location, navigation, and whether a business is open matter beyond finding an offer.
  • Merchant motivations varied: customer acquisition, promotion, or remaining food.
  • Some businesses may not want additional operational work.
  • Users and merchants each need enough value for a marketplace to work.

WHAT STILL NEEDS VALIDATION

  • Whether students would use it, and how often.
  • Whether users would travel for offers and what information they need first.
  • Which offers merchants can provide and how often they can update them.
  • How many users and merchants are needed for useful local coverage.
  • Whether merchants would participate consistently or pay after measurable value.
  • How offers should expire and how sold-out offers can stay trustworthy.

13

Key Product Risks

Marketplace cold start

Too few users can reduce merchant value; too few merchants can reduce user value.

Offer accuracy

Outdated offers could damage user trust.

Merchant effort

Frequent updates could become inconvenient.

Customer attribution

Merchants need a way to understand whether customers came through the platform.

Willingness to pay

Merchant interest does not automatically mean they will pay.

User behaviour

Users may browse offers but choose familiar or nearby restaurants.

Limited-stock offers

Availability can change quickly, creating a mismatch between platform information and reality.

14

Final Product Hypothesis

“There may be an opportunity for a location-based food-offer platform that helps students and young people discover relevant nearby offers while giving food businesses a way to attract customers or promote available offers.”

This remains a hypothesis.

The central unanswered question is whether the marketplace can keep enough useful offers accurate while creating enough value for merchants to participate and eventually pay.

PM

Final Product Thinking Takeaway

The project shifted from a simple discovery interface toward a question about user value, merchant value, information accuracy, and marketplace participation.

INITIAL QUESTION

“How can I help people find food offers?”

REFINED QUESTION

“Can I create a marketplace where users can reliably discover useful nearby offers while merchants can participate without excessive operational effort—and eventually see enough value to pay?”
