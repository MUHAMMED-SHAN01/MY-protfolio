Vehicle Care & Storage

From a Simple Storage Idea to a Product Validation Problem

01

Starting Point

I explored a potential vehicle storage and care service for people who leave their vehicles unused for extended periods. The project began with a simple idea: instead of leaving a vehicle at home, with family or friends, or in standard long-term parking, a customer could hand it over to a dedicated service that stores it and returns it in a usable condition.

At the start, the question was not “How do I build this?” but “Why is this not already a common service?” That question changed the direction of the project.

Discovery goal: understand whether unused vehicles create a strong enough problem for a dedicated, paid service—and for whom.
02

Initial Concept

The initial concept was straightforward: a customer would book the service, hand over the vehicle, have it stored securely, receive periodic checks, and get it returned when needed.

  • Secure vehicle storage
  • Periodic inspection and care
  • Owner updates
  • Vehicle return in usable condition

At the time, I considered different service levels, but they were still hypotheses, not validated product decisions.

03

Assumptions

My initial assumption was:

INITIAL ASSUMPTION

“People who leave their vehicles unused need a dedicated storage and care service.”

That assumption was broad and needed testing. The real question was whether the problem was frequent enough, painful enough, and valuable enough to pay for.

WHAT I NEEDED TO LEARN
Whether people had a meaningful need, how they handled it today, what would make them trust a provider, and whether a managed service could be affordable to customers and feasible to operate.

03A

Potential Customer Segments

These were starting hypotheses to guide discovery, not validated customer groups:

  • Students travelling abroad
  • Professionals relocating
  • Long-term travellers and frequent business travellers
  • Owners of expensive vehicles
  • People without a trusted person nearby

A person away for several months may have different needs from someone whose vehicle is unused for a few weeks. Which segment has the strongest need remains to validate.

↓

“Some people already rely on family/friends, while trust and affordability are important.”

↓
COMPETITIVE RESEARCH

↓

↓

04

Customer Discovery

Early exploratory conversations

Approximately 5 technology professionals

I asked how people would manage a vehicle during a long absence, whether they would trust a person or company to care for it, what might concern them, whether they would pay for dedicated storage, and what they would expect from the service. The aim was to surface early signals and assumptions—not to estimate market demand.

TRUST

Customers may hesitate to hand over a valuable vehicle to an unfamiliar provider.

EXISTING ALTERNATIVES

Family members, friends, home parking and trusted garages can already solve parts of the problem.

AFFORDABILITY

One person earning around ₹20,000/month felt dedicated vehicle storage would not be affordable.

Early signals, not market validation.

OBSERVATION → INSIGHT
Some people already had informal help, and affordability came up. That shifted the question from whether the inconvenience exists to which people lack good alternatives and value a managed service enough to pay.

05

Research Findings

What I learned

  • Trust is important.
  • Informal alternatives exist.
  • Affordability matters.
  • Customer segments may behave differently.
  • Vehicle/storage services already exist in Kerala.

What still needs validation

  • Strongest customer segment
  • Willingness to pay
  • Pricing
  • Required care activities
  • Storage economics
  • Liability requirements
  • Repeat usage
  • Operational feasibility
OBSERVATION

I noticed a Porsche at a relative’s apartment that appeared to have been unused and dusty while its owner was away. I also heard about vehicles left in airport parking for extended periods and needing attention when their owners returned.

LIMITATION

These situations made the problem worth exploring; they do not prove demand. The conversations were small and exploratory, and I have not run a willingness-to-pay test or operated the service.

06

Competitive Research

Research showed that vehicle and storage-related services already exist in Kerala.

BEFORE

“Does vehicle storage exist?”

→
AFTER

“Is there an underserved customer segment looking specifically for managed long-term vehicle storage + periodic care + regular owner updates?”

This reframing makes it clear that the potential gap still requires direct validation. It does not assume a unique opportunity.

Research limitations: public information reviewed showed related services, but direct competitor and customer validation is still needed. I cannot conclude that no provider offers this combination.

07

Reframing

PROBLEM STATEMENT

“For vehicle owners who leave their vehicles unused for extended periods, existing informal and storage options may not always provide convenient, secure and well-maintained vehicle care. However, it is still unclear which customer segments experience this problem strongly enough to pay for a dedicated storage-and-care service.”

08

Proposed Product

Secure StorageKeep the vehicle at a controlled location while its owner is away.
↓
Periodic Check & CareCheck its condition and complete care activities agreed with the customer.
↓
Owner UpdateGive the owner visibility while the vehicle is stored.
↓
Vehicle ReturnPrepare and return the vehicle when the owner needs it.

This proposed service journey combines storage, care and visibility. The activities and service expectations still need to be defined with potential customers.

09

MVP Strategy

WHY NOT BUILD AN APP FIRST?

“The biggest uncertainty was not technological. It was whether customers would actually use and pay for the service.”

Because this is a physical service, a software build would not answer the central demand and willingness-to-pay questions. I would test the service itself first.

  1. Partner or rented facility
  2. Small number of vehicles
  3. Manual booking
  4. Vehicle condition recording
  5. Periodic checks
  6. Customer updates
  7. Vehicle return
“Test the service before investing in infrastructure or software.”

Service packages and pricing remain hypotheses. Storage duration, vehicle type, security, check frequency, care activities, labour, facility cost and willingness to pay would all affect the offer and its economics.

Next validation steps

  1. Speak with more potential customers within specific segments and identify which has the strongest problem.
  2. Test willingness to pay and clarify the service activities customers value.
  3. Talk with potential storage or garage partners about operating costs and feasibility.
  4. Understand liability requirements before offering a service.
  5. Run a small partner-based MVP, then review demand, customer experience, operating cost and repeat interest.
11

Risks

Trust Risk

Customers may hesitate to hand over a valuable asset to an unfamiliar provider.

Affordability Risk

Customers may prefer informal or family/friend alternatives.

Operating Cost Risk

Storage, care, and maintenance may be more expensive than assumed.

Liability Risk

Damage, theft or mechanical issues could create real operational and legal responsibility.

Customer Acquisition Risk

It may be difficult to reach the specific segment that needs the service.

Demand/Seasonality Risk

Need may vary by travel patterns, vehicle type, and local conditions.

12

What I Learned

  1. A problem is not automatically a business opportunity. A valid problem still needs evidence of demand, willingness to pay, and a clear customer segment.
  2. Existing alternatives can be stronger competitors than companies. Friends, family, home parking, and trusted garages can be more competitive than a new service.
  3. Customer segmentation changes the product. The right solution depends on who feels the pain most strongly and whether they can pay.
  4. Trust can be part of the product. Trust is not a side concern; it can determine whether the service is usable at all.
  5. MVP should test the biggest uncertainty. The biggest uncertainty was not technology; it was whether people would choose and pay for the service.
  6. Competitive research can change the product question. Once existing services are identified, the product question shifts from whether storage exists to whether a specific untapped segment exists.
13

Current Status

DISCOVERY / VALIDATION
  • Not launched.
  • No dedicated facility.
  • No paying customer base.
  • No finalized pricing.

The work so far consists of:

Problem exploration → Customer conversations → Real-world observations → Competitive research → Opportunity reframing → MVP planning

I have not yet completed large-scale research, a willingness-to-pay experiment, partner cost interviews, final pricing, or insurance and liability validation. Access to potential segments was limited: a travel agency declined customer access because of liability concerns, and a study-abroad institute did not allow access to students.

14

Next Steps

After testing these assumptions, I would use the evidence to decide whether to scale, modify the service, change the target segment, or stop. The concept remains a hypothesis, not a validated business.

PM

Final Takeaway

“The biggest lesson from this project was learning not to treat the first solution as the product. The product becomes clearer when customer behaviour, existing alternatives, competitive reality, and willingness to pay are investigated first.”
INITIAL QUESTION

“How can I build vehicle storage?”

REFINED QUESTION

“Can I find customers who experience this problem strongly enough to pay for a trusted managed service?”
